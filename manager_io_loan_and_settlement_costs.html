Explanation

In real estate accounting, Loan Costs and Settlement/Closing Costs cannot simply be lumped into your properties as general expenses. They are governed by strict financial rules and must be treated as separate line items on your Balance Sheet. Since you are closing out your QuickBooks history, it is vital to understand exactly what these lines represent so we can transfer their remaining values into Manager.io correctly without losing your historical tax deductions. Here is a breakdown of what these accounts are and how they must be handled in your new system:

  1. Loan Costs & Amortization (Purchase & Refinance)

What this is: These are the fees you paid to your lenders to secure your mortgages (e.g., loan origination fees, underwriting fees, and appraisal fees).

  • The Tax Rule: The IRS forbids you from expensing loan costs all at once in the year you buy or refinance a property. Instead, you must spread the cost out evenly over the life of the loan (e.g., 30 years, which is why your QuickBooks notes say Amort30).
  • The QuickBooks Setup: Your legacy file has a positive asset account tracking the original fee size, and a matching negative account (Amort30) tracking how much of that fee you have successfully written off over the years.

How to Migrate This to Manager.io:

Because these represent an intangible asset that you are actively writing off, you will create two standard accounts inside your Chart of Accounts for each loan:

  1. Create the Accounts globally (Settings > Chart of Accounts):
    • Unamortized Loan Costs - Sawtooth (Asset Group, Investing activities Cash Flow)
    • Loan Cost Amortization Expense (Expense Group)
  2. Initialize the Balances (Settings > Starting Balances > Balance Sheet Accounts):
    • Take the original loan cost asset value from QuickBooks and subtract the cumulative Amort30 negative value.
    • Enter that remaining, unwritten-off net value into Manager.io as a Debit starting balance for that property's loan cost asset.

Every year moving forward, you will write a simple manual Journal Entry to move 1/30th of that original fee out of your asset account and into your Loan Cost Amortization Expense account to claim your annual tax deduction.

  1. Settlement Costs - Purchase & Refinance

What this is: These are the legal and transfer fees paid at the closing table that were not directly charged by the lender (e.g., title insurance, escrow agent fees, recording fees, and transfer taxes).

  • The Purchase Settlement Costs Rule: These costs are considered part of the long-term investment required to acquire the property. They must be added directly to your permanent property cost basis. They do not get their own ongoing account.
  • The Refinance Settlement Costs Rule: Closing costs on a refinance are legally treated exactly like Loan Costs. They must be amortized over the life of the new mortgage loan rather than added to the building's value.

How to Migrate This to Manager.io:

  • For the Purchase Settlement Costs: Check your building's Acquisition Cost starting balance in Manager.io. If these historical closing costs were sitting in a separate account in QuickBooks, add them straight into your building's Acquisition Cost or your Property Land Value inside Manager.io. They will permanently increase your depreciable foundation.
  • For the Refinance Settlement Costs: Combine these numbers directly with your Refinance Loan Costs asset balance described above and amortize them together over the remaining years left on that mortgage.

Theoretically and legally, no, clearing out these loan costs has absolutely zero impact on your Building Basis or your annual building depreciation figures.

In real estate accounting and IRS tax law, your property portfolio is governed by a strict legal firewall that separates structural property costs from mortgage financing costs.

Why Your Building Numbers Stay Completely Untouched

  • The Building Basis Fireball: Your building purchase cost (＄153,920.00 for Sawtooth) is dictated strictly by the physical purchase price of the structure itself plus permanent physical capital improvements.
  • The Loan Cost Separation: Loan fees, appraisal costs, and title closing points are costs paid to acquire the debt (the mortgage), not to acquire the bricks and dirt.

Because the IRS legally demands that you track these two elements on completely separate tracks, deleting your old QuickBooks purchase loan costs will not change your depreciable real estate basis by a single penny. Your building values stay flat, and your standard ＄5,597.00 annual property depreciation deduction remains mathematically pristine.

QUESTION

Let's say a new Rental Property is purchased and incurs similar Settlement and Loan costs again.

How are these accounted for from scratch (with no legacy fixing), in terms of basis values and amortization?

ANSWER

When you buy a new rental property from scratch, you do not create a messy web of sub-accounts. You route all your closing costs into exactly three clean landing locations on Day 1 based on their legal tax classification.

Here is the exact blueprint for how to handle a fresh property purchase in Manager.io:

Landing Spot 1: The Core Real Estate Basis (Capitalized Assets)

What goes here: The purchase price plus all Purchase Settlement Costs (e.g., title insurance, escrow agent fees, deed recording fees, legal fees, and transfer taxes).

  • The Rule: You add these settlement fees directly into the property asset value [5.1]. They are capitalized, meaning they permanently increase your asset basis from day one.
  • How to split it: You take the purchase price + purchase settlement costs, and divide them using your local tax assessor's property appraisal ratio (e.g., 25% Land, 75% Building).
  • Manager.io Location:
    • Create a new standard account for Land Value - Property X under Settings > Chart of Accounts.
    • Create a new Fixed Asset card for Building Value - Property X under the Fixed Assets tab.

Landing Spot 2: Unamortized Loan Costs (Amortized Asset)

What goes here: Every fee charged directly by your lender to secure the mortgage (e.g., loan origination fees, appraisal fees, credit report checks, underwriting fees, and broker points).

  • The Rule: You create a standalone asset account to hold these borrowing fees. You are legally forbidden from expensing them immediately; you must spread them out evenly over the life of your mortgage (typically 30 years).
  • Manager.io Location:
    • Create a standard account named Unamortized Loan Costs - Property X under Settings > Chart of Accounts. Set its group to Assets and Cash Flow to Investing activities.

Landing Spot 3: Immediate Operational Expenses (P&L Write-offs)

What goes here: Intermittent items on your settlement sheet that are pre-paid operational costs rather than transaction fees (e.g., pre-paid interest from the day of closing to the end of the month, or pre-paid homeowner's insurance premiums).

  • The Rule: These are current-year deductions that hit your active cash-flow sheets right away.
  • Manager.io Location:
    • Route these straight to your existing Interest Paid or Insurance Expense accounts inside your Chart of Accounts, making sure to tag your new property Division line-by-line.

  • Hitting your old positive asset rows with a Credit drops them down to zero.

Does QuickBooks Have a Retained Earnings Account Automatically?

Yes, QuickBooks automatically creates a Retained Earnings account for you behind the scenes the moment you set up a company file.

You do not have to manually build it, and you cannot delete it. It is a permanent, hardcoded fixture of the software's structural template.

How QuickBooks Handles It Automatically

In QuickBooks, the Retained Earnings account acts like an automated vacuum cleaner at the end of every fiscal year:

  1. Throughout the year, your income and expenses stay separated on your Profit & Loss statement so you can see your current performance.
  2. The instant the clock strikes midnight on the first day of your new fiscal year, QuickBooks automatically sweeps all the income and expense totals from the year that just ended, combines them into a single net profit or loss number, and dumps that number straight into the Retained Earnings account.
  3. Your income and expense lines are instantly reset back to ＄0.00 for the new year.

How to Find it in Your Legacy File

Depending on how your original file was created or the specific version of QuickBooks you are using, the software will sometimes mask or rename the account on your screen to match your business type. If you go to your Chart of Accounts and look inside your Equity group, you will see it listed under one of these three names:

  • Retained Earnings
  • Net Income (QuickBooks Online will often dynamically display the current year's P&L portion here)
  • Owner’s Equity or Accumulated Retained Earnings

No matter what it is labeled, it is the exact bucket where you would write that rectifying loan-cost adjustment journal entry to safely absorb your old purchase fees and clean up your legacy books!

Purchase and Loan Costs at Property Sale

QUESTION

When the properties are sold, what happens to all the settlement and loan amortizations? Do they get applied as expenses (the remainder)?

ANSWER

Yes, you have hit on one of the most important tax windfalls available when you exit an asset: any remaining unamortized loan balances are fully written off as a 100% immediate expense in the year of the sale.

However, Loan Costs and Purchase Settlement Costs behave completely differently at the time of disposal because they sit on separate sides of that legal accounting firewall.

1. What Happens to Your Loan Costs (The Big Tax Win)

Your loan costs are tied strictly to the mortgage debt. When you sell a rental property, the mortgage is paid off and canceled at the closing table. Because the loan ceases to exist, the IRS allows you to accelerate the remaining timeline and deduct 100% of your leftover unamortized fees immediately against ordinary rental income.

  • How it looks on your taxes:
    • If you have ＄1,861.11 left in your Unamortized Loan Costs - Sawtooth asset row when you sell it, that entire balance instantly flows to your Profit & Loss statement as a standard, current-year operational expense.
  • The structural benefit: This immediate write-off lowers your net passive rental income for that final year, directly shielding your wallet from income taxes.

2. What Happens to Your Purchase Settlement Costs (The Capital Gain Buffer)

Your purchase settlement costs (like title insurance, deed recordings, and legal transfer fees) DO NOT become an expense line on your Profit & Loss sheet.

Instead, because they were added directly to your permanent Building and Land cost basis on Day 1, they work behind the scenes to reduce your taxable capital gains.

  • The Math Breakdown:

Net Sales Price − Total Cost Basis (Purchase Price + Settlement Costs) = Taxable Capital Gain

  • How it shields you: By keeping those historical closing fees inside your property asset cost accounts rather than flushing them out early, your total cost basis stays higher. A higher cost basis automatically shrinks your taxable capital gains window when you cash out, lowering the check you have to hand to the IRS on your sale profit.

The Operational Summary for Your Portfolio

When you sell a rental property, your physical real estate accounts (Land, Building, and Accumulated Depreciation) are used to compute your final Capital Gains Tax, while your remaining financing fee balances (Unamortized Loan Costs) are completely flushed directly onto your P&L sheet as an immediate Ordinary Income Expense Deduction.

QUESTION

So am I correct that for taxation, Settlement costs should be kept from the basis for regular Depreciation, but added to the Purchase price for Capital Gains calculations at sale time?

I'm trying to understand if the Settlement costs are just added to the Building Base before calculating Depreciation, or if the whole purpose of a Settlement cost account is to stop it being amortized.

ANSWER

No, that is actually a slight misconception. Purchase Settlement Costs are included in your regular depreciation base from Day 1. You do not keep them separate.

The entire purpose of tracking Purchase Settlement Costs as a capital asset is to ensure they are written off over time. You do not create a separate, unamortized settlement account at purchase just to hold them until the sale date.

Instead, they follow a simple path based on how they affect your property values and tax deductions:

1. The Rule: They Add to Your Property Value [5.1]

When you buy real estate, the IRS says your true cost isn’t just what you paid the seller—it includes what it legally cost you to clear the deed and close the transaction.

Therefore, things like title insurance, recording fees, attorney fees, and transfer taxes are added directly to your property basis on Day 1.

2. How They Are depreciated (The Building/Land Split)

Because these costs merge into your property value, they get split up using your standard property ratio (e.g., 20% Land, 80% Building):

• The Land Portion (Non-Depreciable): The 20% that lands in your Property Land Value account cannot be depreciated because land lasts forever. This portion sits completely flat on your Balance Sheet and acts purely as a shield to lower your Capital Gains tax when you eventually sell the asset. • The Building Portion (Depreciable): The 80% that lands on your Building Fixed Asset card permanently increases your depreciable building base. This means it is amortized/depreciated over 27.5 years right alongside the brick-and-mortar structure.

They parked the fees in a separate account as a temporary placeholder and forgot to clean it up.

When you buy a property, you completely bypass creating a standalone "Settlement Cost" account. You simply add those closing fees straight to your original purchase price total and split them cleanly:

Total Price + Settlement Costs = Combined Capital Basis

You assign the building percentage to your Fixed Assets Building Card (boosting your yearly $5,597.00 straight-line depreciation) and assign the land percentage to your standard Land Asset Account (boosting your capital gains shield for the future).
